What Is a Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA)?

A Non-Disclosure Agreement (NDA), also known as a confidentiality agreement, is a legally binding contract between two or more parties that outlines confidential information the parties wish to share with each other for certain purposes, but wish to restrict access to by third parties.

NDAs are commonly used in business to protect sensitive information such as trade secrets, business strategies, client lists, proprietary technology, financial data, and other valuable intellectual property.

There are two primary forms of NDAs:

  • Unilateral NDA (One-Way): Only one party discloses confidential information, and the other party agrees to keep it secret. This is the most common type and is what this generator creates.
  • Mutual NDA (Two-Way): Both parties share confidential information and both agree to protect the other's information. These are common in joint ventures and partnerships.

An NDA establishes a confidential relationship between the parties, sets clear expectations about what information must be kept private, and provides legal remedies if the agreement is breached.

Key Clauses in an NDA

A well-drafted NDA should include several essential clauses to be effective and enforceable:

Definition of Confidential Information

Clearly defines what information is considered confidential. This can include documents, data, trade secrets, business plans, customer lists, software code, and any proprietary information shared between the parties.

Obligations of Receiving Party

Specifies how the Receiving Party must handle confidential information, including maintaining secrecy, limiting access to authorized personnel, and using the information only for the stated purpose.

Exclusions from Confidentiality

Defines what information is not covered by the NDA, such as publicly available information, information already known to the Receiving Party, independently developed information, or information disclosed by court order.

Term and Duration

Establishes how long the confidentiality obligations last. This typically ranges from 1 to 5 years, though some agreements may have indefinite terms for trade secrets.

Remedies for Breach

Outlines the consequences if the NDA is breached, including the right to seek injunctive relief, monetary damages, and reimbursement of legal fees incurred in enforcing the agreement.

Governing Law

Specifies which state or jurisdiction's laws will govern the agreement and where any disputes will be resolved. This is important for multi-state or international agreements.

When Do You Need an NDA?

NDAs are essential in many business scenarios. Here are the most common situations where you should consider using a Non-Disclosure Agreement:

  • Hiring Employees: When onboarding new employees who will have access to proprietary information, trade secrets, or sensitive business data.
  • Engaging Freelancers or Contractors: Before sharing project details, codebases, or business strategies with independent contractors or freelance professionals.
  • Investor Pitches: When presenting your business idea, financial projections, or growth strategy to potential investors or venture capitalists.
  • Business Partnerships: When exploring potential partnerships, joint ventures, or mergers that require sharing confidential operational or financial information.
  • Vendor and Supplier Relationships: When third-party vendors need access to your systems, customer data, or internal processes.
  • Product Development: When collaborating with external parties on new products, sharing prototypes, designs, or technical specifications.
  • Due Diligence: During the due diligence phase of acquisitions or investments, where extensive confidential information must be shared.

Types of NDAs

Understanding the different types of NDAs helps you choose the right one for your situation:

Unilateral NDA

A one-way agreement where only one party (the Disclosing Party) shares confidential information, and the other party (the Receiving Party) agrees to protect it. Common in employer-employee, client-vendor, and investor pitch scenarios.

Mutual NDA (Bilateral)

A two-way agreement where both parties share confidential information and both agree to protect the other's disclosures. Commonly used in joint ventures, partnerships, and merger discussions where both sides reveal sensitive data.

Multilateral NDA

An agreement involving three or more parties where at least one party discloses information and multiple parties agree to protect it. This eliminates the need for separate bilateral NDAs between each pair of parties in a multi-party negotiation.
