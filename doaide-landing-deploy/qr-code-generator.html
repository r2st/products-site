What is a QR Code?

A QR (Quick Response) code is a two-dimensional barcode invented in 1994 by Denso Wave, a subsidiary of Toyota. Unlike traditional barcodes that store data in one direction, QR codes encode information both horizontally and vertically, allowing them to hold significantly more data — up to 4,296 alphanumeric characters.

QR codes have become ubiquitous in everyday life. From restaurant menus and product packaging to digital payments and event tickets, they provide a fast and contactless way to share information. Most modern smartphones can scan QR codes directly through their built-in camera app, making them accessible to billions of users worldwide.

Types of QR Codes You Can Create

This tool supports five common QR code types, each designed for a specific use case:

  • URL QR Codes — Link directly to any website. Perfect for business cards, flyers, posters, and product packaging. When scanned, the user's browser opens the encoded URL instantly.
  • Text QR Codes — Encode any plain text message. Useful for sharing short messages, serial numbers, instructions, or any text-based information without requiring an internet connection.
  • WiFi QR Codes — Share WiFi credentials effortlessly. Guests can scan the code and connect to your network automatically without manually typing the password. Supports WPA, WEP, and open networks.
  • vCard QR Codes — Create digital contact cards. When scanned, the contact information (name, phone, email, organization, website) is added directly to the phone's address book.
  • UPI QR Codes — Generate payment QR codes for India's Unified Payments Interface. Include your UPI ID, payee name, and optionally a fixed amount and note for seamless digital payments.

QR Code Best Practices

Follow these tips to ensure your QR codes are effective and scannable:

  • Maintain contrast — QR codes work best with dark modules on a light background. Avoid using low-contrast color combinations that may cause scanning failures.
  • Keep adequate quiet zone — Leave a white margin (quiet zone) around the QR code. This helps scanners distinguish the code from surrounding content.
  • Test before printing — Always scan your QR code with multiple devices and apps before printing or distributing it. Different scanners may handle edge cases differently.
  • Size matters — For print materials, ensure the QR code is at least 2cm x 2cm (0.8in x 0.8in). For billboards or signs viewed from a distance, scale proportionally.
  • Keep data concise — Shorter data produces simpler QR codes that are easier to scan. For URLs, consider using a URL shortener if the link is very long.

How QR Codes Work

QR codes use a matrix of black and white squares (called modules) arranged in a square grid. The code contains several functional patterns that help scanners read the data correctly:

Finder patterns are the three large squares in the corners that help the scanner detect and orient the QR code regardless of rotation. Alignment patterns (smaller squares) help correct distortion when the code is printed on curved surfaces or photographed at an angle.

Timing patterns are alternating black and white modules that help the scanner determine the size of the data matrix. The actual data is encoded using Reed-Solomon error correction, which allows the code to be read even if up to 30% of the surface is damaged or obscured, depending on the error correction level chosen.

QR codes support four error correction levels: L (7% recovery), M (15% recovery), Q (25% recovery), and H (30% recovery). Higher error correction means more redundancy, which results in a denser code but greater resilience to damage.
