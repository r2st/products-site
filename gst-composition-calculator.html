Understanding the GST Composition Scheme in India

The GST Composition Scheme is a simplified taxation framework introduced under the Goods and Services Tax Act for small businesses in India. Designed to reduce the compliance burden on micro and small enterprises, the scheme allows eligible taxpayers to pay tax at a flat rate on their turnover instead of collecting and remitting GST on every individual invoice. This is a significant advantage for businesses that find the regular GST filing process -- monthly returns, invoice-level reporting, and reconciliation -- too complex or resource-intensive to manage.

Who Is Eligible for the Composition Scheme?

To opt for the composition scheme, a business must meet specific turnover thresholds. For manufacturers and traders, the aggregate annual turnover must not exceed Rs. 1.5 crore. For businesses in the special category states of north-eastern India (Arunachal Pradesh, Assam, Manipur, Meghalaya, Mizoram, Nagaland, Sikkim, Tripura) and the states of Jammu & Kashmir, Ladakh, and Uttarakhand, this limit is Rs. 75 lakh. Service providers were brought under the composition umbrella via a notification, and they face a lower threshold of Rs. 50 lakh aggregate turnover.

Several categories of businesses are explicitly excluded from the scheme regardless of turnover. These include suppliers of ice cream and other edible ice, pan masala, aerated water, and tobacco products. Businesses engaged in inter-state outward supplies of goods cannot opt for composition. Similarly, businesses supplying goods through an e-commerce operator that is required to collect tax at source under Section 52 of the CGST Act are not eligible. Casual taxable persons and non-resident taxable persons are also barred from the scheme.

Composition Scheme Tax Rates

The tax rates under the composition scheme are significantly lower than regular GST rates, which is one of the primary advantages for eligible businesses. Manufacturers and traders pay a total of 1% on turnover (split equally as 0.5% CGST and 0.5% SGST/UTGST). Restaurants not serving alcohol pay 5% on turnover (2.5% CGST + 2.5% SGST). Service providers registered under the composition scheme pay 6% on turnover (3% CGST + 3% SGST). It is important to note that composition taxpayers cannot charge GST on their invoices -- the tax is paid out of the business's own margin. This means they issue a "Bill of Supply" rather than a "Tax Invoice."

Key Trade-offs: No ITC, No Interstate Supply

The most significant trade-off of opting for the composition scheme is the loss of Input Tax Credit (ITC). Regular GST-registered businesses can claim credit for the GST paid on their purchases and reduce their output tax liability. Composition dealers cannot claim any ITC, which means the GST paid on raw materials, services, and capital goods becomes a cost to the business. For businesses with high input costs -- such as manufacturers purchasing raw materials at 18% or 28% GST -- this can make the composition scheme less attractive despite the lower headline rate.

Additionally, composition dealers cannot make inter-state supplies. All sales must be intra-state. They also cannot supply through e-commerce platforms that are required to collect TCS. These restrictions limit the business's addressable market and growth avenues, which is why this calculator includes a comparison tool to help you weigh the pros and cons quantitatively.

Filing Requirements: CMP-08 and GSTR-4

One of the major benefits of the composition scheme is simplified compliance. Instead of filing monthly GSTR-1 and GSTR-3B returns (24 filings per year for a regular dealer), composition taxpayers file CMP-08 -- a simple quarterly statement-cum-challan -- and an annual return GSTR-4. CMP-08 is a self-assessed statement where the dealer declares their turnover for the quarter and pays the corresponding tax. The due date for CMP-08 is the 18th of the month following each quarter. GSTR-4, the annual return, is due by 30th April of the following financial year. This brings total filings down to just five per year, a substantial reduction in paperwork and professional costs.

When Should You Choose the Composition Scheme?

The composition scheme works best for businesses that primarily sell to end consumers (B2C), have low input tax costs relative to turnover, operate entirely within one state, and want to minimize compliance overhead. It is less suitable for businesses in the B2B supply chain where buyers need tax invoices to claim their own ITC, or for businesses with significant input costs that would otherwise be recoverable.
