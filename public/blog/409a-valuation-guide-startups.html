409A Valuation: Complete Guide for Startups

If your startup is planning to issue stock options, you need a 409A valuation. Named after Section 409A of the Internal Revenue Code, this independent appraisal determines the fair market value (FMV) of your company's common stock — and it is not optional. Getting it wrong exposes your employees to significant tax penalties. This guide covers what a 409A valuation is, when you need one, how much it costs, and how to navigate the process.

What is a 409A Valuation?

A 409A valuation is an independent appraisal of the fair market value (FMV) of a private company's common stock. It is required under Section 409A of the Internal Revenue Code, which was enacted in 2005 to regulate deferred compensation — including stock options granted to employees.

When a startup grants stock options (either Incentive Stock Options or Non-Qualified Stock Options), those options must have an exercise price (strike price) that is at or above the fair market value of the common stock on the date of the grant. If the strike price is set below FMV, the options are considered "discounted" and the IRS treats them as deferred compensation subject to immediate taxation and penalties.

The 409A valuation determines what that fair market value is. Since private companies do not have publicly traded shares with a market price, an independent valuation is the standard way to establish FMV defensibly.

Key Point

A 409A valuation sets the minimum strike price for stock option grants. You can always set the strike price higher than the 409A value, but never lower. Most companies set the strike price exactly at the 409A value to maximize the benefit to employees.

Common Stock vs. Preferred Stock

The 409A valuation determines the value of common stock, which is what employees receive through option grants. This is almost always lower than the price per share that investors pay for preferred stock in funding rounds, because preferred stock comes with additional rights such as liquidation preferences, anti-dilution protection, and board seats.

The ratio between common stock FMV and the latest preferred stock price varies, but for early-stage startups it is common to see common stock valued at 25–35% of the preferred stock price. This ratio increases as the company matures and approaches an IPO or acquisition.

When Do You Need a 409A Valuation?

There are several situations that trigger the need for a new or updated 409A valuation:

Trigger Event When to Get 409A Consequence of Skipping
First stock option grant Before granting any options All options at risk of being improperly priced
New funding round closes Before granting options post-round Old valuation is stale; options may be underpriced
12 months since last valuation Before the anniversary date Valuation expires; no safe harbor for new grants
Significant revenue milestone Before next option grant Material event invalidates prior valuation
M&A discussions or LOI received Immediately — do not grant options Extreme risk of underpricing; potential IRS scrutiny
Major pivot or product launch Before next option grant if value materially changed Valuation may no longer reflect current FMV
Critical Rule

Never grant stock options after a material event without updating your 409A valuation first. A material event is any occurrence that significantly changes the company's value — a funding round, acquisition offer, major customer win, or significant revenue change. Options granted with a stale 409A carry real risk.

How the 409A Valuation Process Works

A qualified 409A valuation provider uses one or more of three standard valuation methods to determine the fair market value of your common stock:

The Three Valuation Methods

Method How It Works Best For
Asset Approach Values the company based on the net value of its assets (tangible and intangible) minus liabilities Pre-revenue startups with minimal operating history; asset-heavy companies
Market Approach Compares the company to similar publicly traded companies or recent acquisitions using multiples (revenue, EBITDA, users) Companies with revenue and comparable public peers; later-stage startups
Income Approach Projects future cash flows and discounts them to present value using a risk-adjusted discount rate (DCF analysis) Companies with predictable revenue streams and reliable financial projections

Most 409A valuations for startups use the market approach as the primary method, supplemented by the income approach for companies with sufficient revenue history. Pre-revenue startups often rely primarily on the asset approach combined with a backsolve method that works backward from the most recent funding round price.

The Backsolve Method

The backsolve (or OPM backsolve) method is particularly common for venture-backed startups. It uses the most recent preferred stock price from a funding round as an anchor, then works backward to determine what the common stock would be worth given the different rights attached to preferred vs. common shares. This typically involves an Option Pricing Model (OPM) or Probability-Weighted Expected Return Method (PWERM).

Discount for Lack of Marketability (DLOM)

Since private company shares cannot be freely traded on a public exchange, a Discount for Lack of Marketability (DLOM) is applied to the valuation. This discount typically ranges from 20% to 40% for early-stage startups and decreases as the company approaches an IPO. The DLOM reflects the fact that an employee cannot easily sell their shares compared to publicly traded stock.

What Happens If You Skip the 409A?

The penalties for non-compliance with Section 409A are severe — and they fall on the employees, not the company:

IRS Penalties for 409A Non-Compliance

20% additional federal income tax on the full value of the deferred compensation (the spread between exercise price and FMV), applied immediately upon vesting — not at exercise. Plus a premium interest rate penalty calculated from the date the options first vested. These penalties apply to every option grant that lacks a compliant 409A valuation.

While the tax penalties technically fall on employees, the practical consequences for the company are significant:

  • Employee lawsuits: Employees can sue the company for negligence in failing to obtain a proper 409A valuation, seeking damages for the penalties they incurred.
  • Hiring and retention damage: Word spreads quickly in startup ecosystems. A 409A compliance failure makes it harder to recruit top talent with equity compensation.
  • Due diligence red flags: During M&A or IPO due diligence, missing or non-compliant 409A valuations are a significant red flag that can delay or derail deals.
  • State tax consequences: California and other states may impose additional penalties on top of the federal 20% tax.

Step-by-Step 409A Valuation Process

Here is what the end-to-end 409A valuation process looks like for a typical startup:

  1. Choose a provider: Select a qualified, independent 409A valuation firm. Ensure they have experience with your stage and industry. The provider must be independent — they cannot have a financial interest in your company.
  2. Gather required documents: Prepare your cap table, articles of incorporation, financial statements (P&L, balance sheet), financial projections, most recent funding term sheets, and any board-approved option pool details.
  3. Complete the questionnaire: Most providers send a detailed questionnaire about your business: revenue, growth rate, industry, competitive landscape, key milestones, and future plans. Be thorough and accurate — this drives the valuation.
  4. Provider performs analysis: The valuation firm applies the appropriate methodology (market, income, or asset approach), calculates the equity value, allocates it across share classes, and applies DLOM.
  5. Review the draft report: You will receive a draft report showing the FMV per share of common stock. Review it for accuracy of inputs (cap table, financials) and ask questions about the methodology.
  6. Receive final report: Once you approve, the firm issues the final 409A valuation report. This report provides safe harbor protection for option grants made at or above the stated FMV.
  7. Set strike price and grant options: Use the 409A value as the minimum strike price for all new option grants. Record the grant in your cap table and issue option agreements to employees.

409A Safe Harbor: What It Means and How to Get It

Safe harbor is a legal protection that shifts the burden of proof to the IRS if they challenge your stock option pricing. Without safe harbor, the IRS only needs to show your valuation was "unreasonable." With safe harbor, they must prove it was "grossly unreasonable" — a much higher bar.

There are three ways to achieve safe harbor:

  • Independent appraisal (most common): Obtain a valuation from a qualified independent appraiser. This is the standard approach used by virtually all venture-backed startups.
  • Binding formula: Use a formula consistently applied to determine FMV (e.g., a multiple of book value). Rarely used by startups.
  • Illiquid startup safe harbor: For companies less than 10 years old with no publicly traded securities and no reasonable anticipation of a change of control or IPO within 90 days, a valuation by a "qualified individual" (not necessarily a firm) with relevant experience can provide safe harbor. This option is more accessible but provides weaker protection.

Key Factors That Affect Your 409A Value

Several factors influence where your common stock FMV lands:

  • Most recent funding round: The preferred stock price from your latest priced round is the strongest signal. The common stock is then discounted from this based on the preferred vs. common rights differential.
  • Revenue and growth rate: Companies with strong, growing revenue will have higher valuations. ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue) is the key metric for SaaS companies.
  • Market comparables: How similar public companies and recent M&A transactions are valued affects your valuation through the market approach.
  • DLOM (Discount for Lack of Marketability): Typically 20–40% for early-stage startups; decreases as IPO or acquisition becomes more likely.
  • Time since last funding: A recent funding round provides a strong anchor. As time passes without a new round, the valuation becomes more dependent on financial performance.
  • Stage and risk profile: Pre-revenue companies carry more risk (and lower valuations) than companies with established revenue and customers.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long is a 409A valuation valid?

A 409A valuation is generally valid for 12 months from the valuation date, provided there is no material event that significantly changes the company's value. Material events include a new funding round, significant revenue changes, M&A discussions, or a major pivot. After a material event, you need a new 409A before granting options.

How much does a 409A valuation cost?

Costs range from $1,000–$5,000 for online platforms to $5,000–$25,000+ for traditional and Big 4 firms. Early-stage startups with simple cap tables can use affordable online providers like DoAide, while complex later-stage companies may need boutique or large firms.

What is 409A safe harbor and why does it matter?

Safe harbor shifts the burden of proof to the IRS if they challenge your option pricing. With safe harbor, the IRS must prove your valuation was "grossly unreasonable" rather than just "unreasonable." The most common way to achieve safe harbor is through an independent appraisal from a qualified valuation firm.

What happens if I issue stock options without a 409A valuation?

If options are issued below fair market value, employees face a 20% additional federal income tax on the deferred compensation plus a premium interest rate penalty. The penalties apply upon vesting, not exercise. While the company does not pay directly, the liability exposure and employee impact are severe.

Does a new funding round automatically invalidate my existing 409A?

Yes. A priced funding round is a material event that invalidates your existing 409A valuation. You must obtain a new 409A before granting any additional stock options after the round closes. Most companies include a new 409A valuation in their post-closing checklist.
